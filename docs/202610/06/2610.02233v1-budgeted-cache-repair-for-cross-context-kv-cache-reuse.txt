BUDGETED CACHE REPAIR
FOR CROSS-CONTEXT KV-CACHE REUSE
Haeyong Kang∗
Chang D. Yoo
Duksung Women’s University
KAIST
ABSTRACT
Cross-context KV-cache reuse predicts a shared segment’s keys and values under a
new prefix instead of recomputing them, and has been reported to do so without
quality loss. We find otherwise, and identify two problems. (1) A hidden cost:
on MMLU and GSM8K, reuse costs substantial accuracy. (2) A decision at the
wrong unit: no rule for deciding whether to reuse a cache removes that cost. What
does help is choosing which parts of the cache to recompute, and the value of
choosing well falls as the unit of choice grows: informed selection removes 49.5%
of the cache error beyond chance at single rows (one token’s keys and values),
10.6% at 64-token chunks, and nothing at the level of whole calls. Budgeted Cache
Repair (BCR) acts at the unit where selection still pays. It drafts two tokens from
the assembled cache, ranks cache rows by the attention those tokens pay them, and
recomputes a fixed budget of rows exactly, in one of three layouts. The cost is paid
rather than predicted away, and the draft that fails as a gate succeeds as a selector.
BCR restores GSM8K to dense-prefill accuracy while still serving most calls from
cache, and its best layout outperforms every reuse baseline’s mean in the reference
grid. The draft also beats a coin-flip selector at the same budget—a control prior
evaluations lack.

INTRODUCTION
A multi-agent LLM system processes the same context again and again. When three agents and an
aggregator each receive the same question and each other’s outputs, the question is prefilled four
times and the first answer three times. This redundancy motivates cross-context KV-cache reuse
(Kwon et al., 2023; Gim et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2025): rather than recomputing a segment’s keys and
values under every new prefix, a reuse method estimates whether the representations computed earlier
are still valid. KVCOMM (Ye et al., 2025) does this with an online pool of anchors—segments stored
together with the deviation a prefix change induced in them—and reports over 70% cache reuse and
up to 7.8× faster time-to-first-token with no measurable quality loss.
A reused cache is not always safe to serve, so every reuse system needs a rule for deciding when
its prediction can be trusted. CacheBlend (Yao et al., 2025), Figure 1(a), sidesteps the question by
recomputing selected tokens instead of trusting the seams. KVCOMM instead applies a gate: once
per reused segment, before generation begins, it thresholds the entropy of the segment’s similarity
to the stored anchors (§3). The gate’s defining limitation, Figure 1(b), is that it decides before any
information about the answer exists: it can inspect only the prompt and the predicted cache, so it
uses the cache’s geometry as a surrogate for whether reuse will change the answer.
We find that this prompt-side surrogate is poorly aligned with the errors reuse actually causes. Reuse
costs substantial accuracy on two of three benchmarks, and the cost survives a check for anchor-pool
warm-up. Tightening the gate does not remove it, and neither does choosing which calls to reuse:
across five call-level allocation policies, including a random baseline, accuracy tracks the amount
of reuse rather than the policy. Nor does deferring the decision until generation has begun, the fix
that cache eviction uses (Li et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025): our generation-time
gate OPENGATE, which decides from two draft tokens, loses 6.7 points to DENSE on 300 matched

This motivates a different approach: repair part of the cache instead of trusting all of it. Budgeted
Cache Repair (BCR) uses generation itself to find the parts of a reused cache most worth repairing.
It drafts two tokens from the assembled cache, ranks cache rows by how much attention those tokens
pay them, and recomputes exactly a fixed budget of the highest-ranked rows. We study three repair
layouts, illustrated in Figure 1(c): fixed chunks, grown spans, and individual rows (§3). BCR pays
the hidden cost directly, and pays it at a unit where informed selection can still reduce it.
Our contributions are as follows.
• Budgeted Cache Repair. We introduce BCR, a generation-guided method that uses a two-token
draft to select, and then exactly recompute, a fixed budget of KV rows, in three repair layouts: fixed
chunks, grown spans, and individual rows.
• Accuracy recovery under reuse. BCR recovers most of the accuracy reuse costs, restoring
GSM8K to dense-prefill accuracy while keeping most of the reuse, and on each benchmark of the
reference grid its best layout leads every reuse baseline’s mean, KVCOMM and CacheBlend alike.
Recovery is task-dependent: gains on MMLU are limited, and HumanEval has little degradation to
recover.
• What determines the error of reuse. The value of informed selection falls as the unit of decision
grows, from single rows to chunks to whole calls, and the cost itself is workload- or backbone-
specific.

RELATED WORK
Reuse across contexts.
Prefix caching (Kwon et al., 2023) reuses a cache only under an exact prefix
match. Prompt Cache (Gim et al., 2024) extends reuse to position-agnostic segments, and CacheBlend
(Yao et al., 2025), Figure 1(a), repairs independently cached chunks by recomputing selected tokens.
KVCOMM (Ye et al., 2025), Figure 1(b), takes a different route: it predicts the deviation a prefix
change causes, using an online anchor pool, and decides per segment whether to reuse the cache.
We study this formulation and ask whether such prediction preserves accuracy, including after the
anchor pool has warmed up. Multi-agent pipelines (Du et al., 2024; Zhuge et al., 2024) make such
reuse frequent; AgentPrune (Zhang et al., 2025) attacks the same cost from the communication side.
Cache-compression methods face a related choice of what to keep, using positional rules (Xiao et al.,
2024), reconstruction (Zweiger et al., 2026; Kim et al., 2025), or attention-based scores (Li et al.,
2024; Zhang et al., 2023; Cai et al., 2025; Feng et al., 2025); recent work shows that such input-side
criteria can be misaligned with task performance (Wang et al., 2025). BCR differs from all of these

in what it decides: it fixes a repair budget and chooses what to repair, rather than whether to trust
the cache as a whole. Like CacheBlend it pays for selective recomputation, but it applies the idea
to cross-context reuse and studies how the unit of repair changes its value; Figure 1(c) draws the
contrast.
Selecting what to recompute.
Recent work improves selective recomputation by ranking tokens
with attention from the user query (Teng et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026) or from a small model that
approximates the target (Yang et al., 2025), and reports substantial gains at matched budgets. Draft
passes that generate tokens before committing (Leviathan et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025) likewise
motivate generation-time signals for selection. These methods ask which tokens to select within a
fixed formulation. We isolate two factors instead: what informed selection is worth relative to an
uninformed baseline, and how that worth depends on the unit of selection. We therefore compare
informed and random selection at the same repair budget, and vary the unit from whole calls to
chunks to individual cache rows. That controlled view is the focus of BCR.

BUDGETED CACHE REPAIR
The reuse problem.
A request runs on a directed acyclic graph of N agents. Agent i’s prompt is
assembled from a private system prompt Si, the shared task T, and the outputs of its predecessors, so
in the fully connected configuration studied here a segment such as T is prefilled N+1 times. Let
C(x | P) denote the KV cache of a token span x computed under prefix P, one row per token. Every
entry of that cache depends on P through attention, so exact reuse requires an identical prefix—the
condition prefix caching assumes (Kwon et al., 2023). KVCOMM (Ye et al., 2025) relaxes it by
predicting the dependence. For a segment x already seen under some prefix P, it stores an anchor:

segment’s deviation is approximated by a similarity-weighted combination of stored ∆’s, added
elementwise to the cache.

An agent’s prompt contains several such segments—the task, each predecessor’s output—
and each is decided separately: trust the approximation (reuse) or pay a full prefill for that segment
(dense). KVCOMM decides before generating any token, from an input-side statistic. Let dj be the
L2 distance in value space between the candidate segment and anchor j, collect the distances over the

The repair.
Predicting a cache instead of computing it costs accuracy, and no setting of the gate
avoids that cost; §4.1 measures both. Reusing fewer caches, and choosing which, does not avoid it
either: §4.2 rules that out and prices what choosing well is worth at each unit. What remains is to
repair part of the reused cache rather than decide whether to trust all of it. As Figure 1(c) illustrates,
BCR recomputes a fixed budget ρ of the cache’s rows exactly and reuses the rest. Whenever

The layouts.
Spending a budget at all recovers 7.9 of the 13.7 accuracy points reuse costs on
GSM8K, and the rule that places the budget recovers 5.4 more (§4.3). How large the budget should
be, Table 1(a)’s coin-drawn sweep cannot resolve: at ρ=0.20 its seeds span 11.3 accuracy points,
wider than the 82.7 −77.3 gap they bracket. What it leaves open is the layout the budget is spent in,
and we test three, coarsest first:
• BCR-chunk divides the cache into fixed 64-token chunks and recomputes the highest-scoring ρ of
them. Spans have a fixed length and sit on a grid.
• BCR-grow drops the fixed unit: it seeds on the best-scoring row and extends the span outward
while a neighbour scores at least τ of the seed. Lengths follow the score surface, and positions are
not aligned to anything.
• BCR-single drops grouping altogether and spends the budget one row at a time, the finest unit the
budget admits.
One budget, three units.
Unqualified, BCR means all three. Table 1(b) puts their row counts
within 12% of the smallest (154 against 138 on HumanEval, the widest of the three; 9.4% on MMLU
and 4.7% on GSM8K), so the three share one budget and differ in the unit they spend it in; comparing
them isolates the unit, not the spend. Table 1(c) prices the budget in time: 190.1 ms to the first token,
against reuse’s 72.9 and DENSE’s 542.7.

EXPERIMENTS
The measurement setting.
GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) and MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021)
run on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024) and are scored by exact match and letter
matching respectively; HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021) runs on Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct (Hui
et al., 2024) and is scored by unit tests. Two configurations recur, and every result is labelled with
the one it uses. The reference configuration follows the released implementation: roles cycle rather
than stay pinned, and the shipped question budgets—150, 153 and 160 items—are run at 2 to 5
agents in Table 3. Its three-MathSolver GSM8K cell, with N+1 = 4 calls per request, is where
§4.1 begins: 300 problems, the first 60 of which also serve to sweep γ. The pinned configuration
assigns five agents fixed roles, over 300 matched GSM8K and MMLU problems in Table 5 or the
150 problems of Table 4(a); pinning concentrates divergence at the entry node, where §4.1 measures
it. Statistical significance in this paper comes from these single runs over distinct items, never
from Table 3. Decoding is greedy throughout, with 512 maximum new tokens, on one NVIDIA
A100 80GB; anchor-pool hyperparameters stay at the repository defaults. App. G.1 gives the full
environment and scoring rules.
The statistical protocol.
Within each comparison, every condition runs the same problems with the
same decoding parameters, so every comparison is paired. We report the discordant split b/c before
any p-value: b counts problems solved only by the first-named condition, c those solved only by the
other, unless a table’s caption fixes the orientation for its rows. An exact McNemar test on these
paired disagreements then asks whether they favour one condition more often than chance would.

4.1
WHAT REUSE COSTS
We measure two things in order: whether predicting the cache instead of recomputing it costs
accuracy, and whether that cost can be blamed on the rule that decides when to trust a prediction—the
gate of Equation (2). Both measurements compare the same problems solved two ways, under the
matched-pair protocol above.
The cost of reuse.
Table 2 puts the cost at −4.3 points on 300 matched GSM8K problems in
the reference configuration (p = 0.035 on 33 discordant pairs); pinned, the cost is 13.7 (§4.3).
Unconditional reuse loses 7.7 (p = 0.001), and OPENGATE, the answer-side gate of App. A, loses
6.7 (p = 0.009). No setting of the gate, and no replacement we built for it, avoids the cost of
predicting the cache; recomputing part of the prediction does (§3), while changing the decision about
the whole cache does not. At γ = 0.3, reuse loses 23 problems that DENSE solves but gains 10 that
DENSE misses, Table 2’s 23/10: 33 problems change outcome for a net change of only 13, or 4.3
points. The approximated caches produce different answers, not occasional slips.

0.537, against 0.625 to 0.679 in later blocks, because a dense pass is what materializes an anchor:

The gate sweep.
If the loss came from misplaced trust in predictions, tightening the rule should
recover quality. Table 2 sweeps it, and Table 9 sweeps it more finely on the first 60 problems, with
latencies. The setting γ = 1.0 is not an extrapolation but the exact “never refuse” policy, since
H(s) ≤log2 n makes the test unfireable there—which App. G.3 uses as a check that the setting
took effect. Every setting stays below DENSE, and the cost deepens as reuse rises: −4.3, −6.7 and
−7.7 accuracy points, at p = 0.035, 0.009 and 0.001. The gate moves the operating point along
the one reuse–accuracy line that Figure 2(b) draws; it does not lift the line. The loss lives in the
approximation itself, not in the rule that decides when to apply it.
The agent-count grid.
Table 3 repeats the comparison at two to five agents, and DENSE leads
KVCOMM in all eight MMLU and GSM8K cells. No single cell is significant at the repository’s
own sample sizes: Table 4(d) gives all twelve splits; the largest, GSM8K at two agents, is 17/9,
p = 0.169. Pooling the counts is not a valid test (§4), so the grid gives direction, not magnitude;
significance rests on the single n=300 runs.
The same grid puts the repair at DENSE’s ceiling, where the reuse baselines stop short. In Table 3’s
means—descriptive, since the four cells re-run the same problems—BCR-chunk takes GSM8K at
84.7, and BCR-grow takes MMLU at 75.3 and HumanEval at 88.4: level with DENSE’s 84.5, 75.0
and 86.1, and clear of KVCOMM’s 80.8, 72.5 and 85.8 and of CacheBlend’s best, 83.7, 71.7 and
86.4. The three layouts spend one budget, and which of them leads depends on the benchmark (§4.3);
some layout beats KVCOMM in all twelve cells and each CacheBlend pairing in eleven.
The comparison matches conditions on budget, and Table 3’s GSM8K cell re-runs to the printed
scores exactly. MMLU does not. Its analysis agent splices a live, uncached en.wikipedia.org
fetch into the prompt, so every condition—DENSE included—depends on what Wikipedia said that
day. Disabling retrieval makes the pipeline exactly reproducible, and that offline regime, documented
in App. G.2, is what Table 3’s MMLU column now reports. Measured offline, the cost is 2.0 to
2.7 points—three or four problems of 153 in Table 4(d)’s counts, none significant. The direction is
unchanged, and the caveat applies to any MMLU number measured with roles cycling, the reference
implementation’s included.

WHAT THE REPAIR RECOVERS
DENSE prefills every segment and is the ceiling in the sense that matters: no budget ρ < 1 can
improve on ρ=1. KVCOMM is the method under study. CacheBlend (Yao et al., 2025), reproduced

published selective-recomputation competitor, and a coin at the same budget is the control. The gate is
γ=0.3 on the Llama benchmarks and γ=1.0 on HumanEval; every repair takes ρ=0.20, with τ=0.25
for BCR-grow. Within each comparison the selection condition is therefore the only variable, with
one exception: GSM8K’s CacheBlend pairings run at γ=1.0 against the other conditions’ 0.3, a
mismatch that App. D shows runs against CacheBlend.

one line. Any reuse system that concatenates per-segment IDs pays this tax.

At a budget of a fifth of the cache, BCR returns GSM8K to DENSE while

Where it recovers little, on MMLU, almost half of the gap is one no budget could reach, because it is
not in the cache.
5
DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
Cross-context KV-cache reuse is reported to be lossless. On two of three benchmarks we find
otherwise. With roles pinned, reuse costs 13.7 points on GSM8K and 14.3 on MMLU (9.3–10.3 after
re-grading), while HumanEval shows no measurable loss. Nor can it be avoided by choosing which
calls to reuse: the value of informed selection falls from 49.5% to 10.6% to 0 as the unit of decision
grows. It can be repaired: a fixed budget of exact recomputation lets BCR restore GSM8K to DENSE
accuracy while keeping most of the reuse (reuse rate 0.629), and two of the three layouts reach that
level; on every benchmark of the reference grid the best layout leads every reuse baseline’s mean. On
MMLU, however, only part of the gap is in the cache at all.
The key finding is that where the decision is made matters as much as whether it is. Our evidence
spans three benchmarks and two backbones, but a single topology and a single chunk size. BCR also
trades latency for accuracy: its 2.85× speedup in Table 1(c) is below reuse’s 7.63×, a trade App. J

The policy.
On every call KVCOMM serves from an assembled cache, BCR inserts three steps
before generation. A prompt-side score would be free at that moment—KVCOMM’s own gate is one
such score—but any such score ranks cached rows by the geometry of the cache rather than by what
the answer turns out to need. We therefore spend k decode steps on a draft instead.
1. Draft. Generate k=2 tokens on the assembled cache and keep the last k query rows. The draft is then
discarded: every layer is truncated back to its pre-draft length and the token counter restored. Repair and real
generation therefore both begin from the cache the call was served with. The k drafted tokens are decoded a
second time, now against the repaired cache, and need not come back the same. OPENGATE handles its draft
the other way: it keeps the draft and continues from the returned cache (App. I). That is the case in which
“no extra forward pass” holds; for BCR it does not. The served cache stops one token short of the prompt
(T = |x| −1), so at k=2 the kept rows are the queries of the prompt’s final token and of the first generated
one: one prompt-side, one answer-side. We fix k=2 and never sweep it, so §4.3’s estimate bounds what one
answer-side query row is worth, not what a longer draft would be.
2. Score. The rule of §3, with grouped-query keys expanded to query heads. Scoring a chunk by the mean of its
rows’ scores keeps a short trailing chunk from being penalised for its length, and unlike an eviction mask the
selection cannot differ per head.
3. Repair. Recompute the highest-scoring ρ of the chunks exactly against the cache, and resume.
The skip rule, the recompute path, the budget’s denominator and the correctness check are stated
once in App. B, with Algorithm 1.
The random control.
Repairing chunks and choosing them are separable, and §4.2 predicts the
choice is worth much less at the chunk unit than at the row unit. We therefore run the same policy
with the chunks picked by coin at an identical budget. Whatever separates the two rows is the draft’s
contribution; whatever they share belongs to repair itself. The coin condition also skips the draft
entirely, so it spends k fewer decode steps per repaired call: a null between the rows would have been
a dominance rather than a tie. A single coin run had suggested such a null; the five-seed control of
§4.3 replaces it, so the draft is not free to remove, and App. C prices what the timed rows omit.

Correctness.
Selecting every position must reproduce DENSE. Compared tensor by tensor, the two
agree up to numerical precision: layer 0 is exact, and the relative difference grows with depth to a
few percent at 512 tokens, because this path accumulates attention in float32 while the model’s fused
kernel does not. The functional check is the one that decides, and it passes—greedy generation from
the two caches is token-identical over 24 tokens. We report the tensor-level discrepancy rather than
tune it away.
The seam, and what the budget counts.
We install the policy by wrapping model.generate,
the same seam OPENGATE uses, and for the same reason: every state mutation in KVCOMM’s reuse
path happens after generate returns. We rank chunks over the whole cached prefix rather than over
the reused segment alone, because segment boundaries are not visible at that seam. Budget spent on a
chunk that was already exact therefore has no effect, and the row counts we report include it.

LATENCY, AND WHAT THE REPAIR COSTS
Latencies come from n=60 runs with one process on an idle GPU (Table 9); the larger runs were
executed sequentially, and we report no headline latencies from them—the one per-problem timing
taken from an n=150 run is reported in App. H, where it does not pass a paired test.
Latency on the workload.
Against DENSE’s 101.5 ms, every reuse setting lands between 78.8 and
82.7 ms—a 1.2–1.3× improvement, flat in γ because the calls converted at high γ are the ambiguous
ones whose cache assembly costs nearly what it replaces. The prefill being replaced is small: GSM8K
questions average 59 tokens against roughly 1,000 generated across four agent calls, so prefill is
≈0.4% of a request and the 22 ms recovered is a fifth of it. End-to-end, latency rises rather than falls,
and rises with reuse—28.33 s DENSE against 32.00 s at the default gate, rising to 36.78 s—which
puts the per-call cost of anchor matching and offset application near 0.9–2.1 s against the 22 ms of
prefill it removes.

Placement at a fixed budget.
Everything so far varies how much cache is recomputed. Table 12
varies only how it is arranged: the budget stays round(ρM) and the cap on one contiguous repaired
span moves from a single token up to 64. The result is a step rather than a slope. Repairing individual
rows scores above DENSE in both cells; allowing spans to grow at all costs most of that immediately,
and growing them further changes nothing—the 8-, 16- and 64-token caps land within one problem of
each other, and their realised mean span lengths sit within 0.2 tokens because τ, not the cap, is what
stops growth. Pooled over all four agent counts (the two- and four-agent cells are those of Table 12;
the other two are not tabulated), BCR-single leads the 64-token cap 47/27; the naive McNemar p is
0.027, but the four cells re-run the same problems (§4) and the item-level correction returns p = 0.13.
The table also separates the unit from the rule that picks it. CacheBlend at the matched cost is a
single-row layout too, ranking rows by their layer-1 value deviation instead of by our draft score, and
it lands with the 1-token cap—one cell each way, neither significant. Two unrelated selectors put the
same budget on single rows and land together, which is §4.2’s conclusion reached in task space.
Two qualifications. First, the comparison the body’s claims rest on is against BCR-chunk, and there
the two are indistinguishable (25/26 pooled), exactly as both are indistinguishable from DENSE:
BCR-single is an equivalent way to spend the budget on this workload rather than a better one, and
its advantage over BCR-grow does not carry to MMLU. Second, the ordering across the sweep is
the evidence rather than any single contrast (§4.3)—read with Table 11’s caution about single-run
orderings, though the two settings whose caps do not bind returned identical answers on all 150
problems, which bounds this scale’s run-to-run noise at zero.
Implementations.
The accuracy tables in the body were produced with the earlier draft-scored,
layer-serial repair; the timing rows use the coin-selected, fused one. The two are functionally
equivalent—repairing every chunk reproduces DENSE’s greedy generation token-for-token under
both—but not equal in cost: the draft-scored repair spends k=2 decode steps per repaired call before
any repair begins, and Table 10 does not charge for them. Since the control of §4.3 shows the draft
contributes to accuracy, the timed rows are to be read as the cost of the coin condition; the draft-scored
configuration adds the k decode steps on top of them.

E
CELLS ABOVE DENSE PREFILL
The cells themselves.
BCR is above DENSE in the avg column of all three benchmarks, not only
in the sixteen cells §4.3 counts; BCR-chunk’s GSM8K margin there is +0.2, less than a single
problem in 150. Two things produce them, and neither is the repair exceeding DENSE.
Resolution.
The first is resolution. The pooled splits behind the cells above DENSE are level—
26/25 and 30/30 on GSM8K, 49/47 on MMLU—and the gaps are one to five problems out of 150
to 160. BCR-grow on GSM8K is the one layout whose pooled split against DENSE is not level, at
20/40, and it falls below. The grid’s one significant cell (§4.3) is against KVCOMM, the comparison
those splits are drawn for, not against DENSE.
Tokenization.
The second is that BCR is not DENSE minus some work. The reuse path concatenates
per-segment token ids, and that sequence need not equal the chat template’s tokenization of the same
text, so a repaired call is a different input rather than a degraded one. §4.3 measures that term directly:
on MMLU it costs 6.7 of the 14.3-point gap, and on GSM8K the entry tokenizations coincide and it
vanishes. A term that can cost 6.7 points on one benchmark can land the other way by one or two on
another, which is enough to put a repaired run on either side of DENSE with nothing having been
improved. On HumanEval the first account carries the cells on its own: every condition’s average sits
inside four points and the reuse-matched CacheBlend pairing is above DENSE while plain KVCOMM
is below—an ordering that follows from no property of any repair, ours or theirs. One such cell
arose earlier in this work and traced to a misapplied rule: with one CacheBlend rule misapplied the
reproduction scored above DENSE on this same benchmark, and correcting the rule returned it to
level (App. H).
Could the draft exceed DENSE?
Neither account needs the repair to exceed DENSE, and there
is an obvious mechanism by which it could—worth stating because we can rule it out. BCR ranks
cached rows by the attention queries of a k-token draft (§3). At k=2 that is one prompt-side query
row and one answer-side one (App. A.2)—evidence of the kind §1 faults the gate for never having—
so a repaired run consults something DENSE does not, and one might expect it to place its exact
recomputation better than exactness-everywhere places it.
The coin control of §4.3 settles it. The draft’s signal is worth something—about 5.4 points over five
coin seeds—but k decides only where a fixed budget ρ < 1 of exact recomputation lands; it cannot
make that subset better than recomputing everything. On MMLU the coin means 50.0% over five
seeds against the draft-scored repair’s 54.7, but both sit far below DENSE’s 66.0 and neither is a
candidate for exceeding it.

WHAT THE REPAIR BUYS, AND WHAT NO BUDGET REACHES
Time to first token.
On a 4096-symbol segment DENSE prefills in 542.7 ms, KVCOMM assembles
in 72.9 and BCR at ρ=0.20 in 190.1—7.63× and 2.85× against DENSE (Table 1(c)). The premium
19


Preprint. Under review.
BCR pays over plain reuse is the price of the recomputed rows and is linear in the budget: 46, 64,
117, 182 and 231 ms at ρ = 0.05 through 0.40, for speedups of 4.5× down to 1.8× (App. C). The
advantage is structural rather than constant— assembling a cache from anchors does not scale with
the segment the way prefilling it does—so it grows with the shared segment and vanishes without
one.
Throughput.
We do not measure it, and on these workloads it could not separate the conditions.
GSM8K’s shared segment averages 59 tokens against roughly 1,000 generated across four agent
calls, so the prefill reuse targets is ≈0.4% of a request; the end-to-end columns move by 0.6–3.3%
for plain reuse and by under 6% for any repair row, buried under a generation that is identical in
every condition (App. C). A throughput claim needs a workload where prefill is a larger share of the
request—long shared segments, or many calls per request—and that workload is not among the three
here.
The tokenization tax.
The reuse path concatenates per-segment token ids, and that sequence need
not equal the chat template’s tokenization of the same text, so a repaired call is a different input
rather than a degraded one. The token-matched control of §4.3 prices it: on MMLU it is 6.7 of
the 14.3-point gap (66.0 −59.3 in Table 5(a)); on GSM8K the entry tokenizations coincide and it
vanishes, which is why repair recovers nearly the whole gap there. We price it on MMLU only. It is
not a property of our method: any reuse system that concatenates per-segment ids pays it.
The two together.
No budget reaches the tax, because it is not in the cache but in which tokens the
cache is of. That bounds what the latency premium can buy on a benchmark where the tax is large:
on MMLU at most 7.6 of the 14.3 points are inside the cache at all, and BCR recovers 3.0 of those
while paying the same 2.85× it pays on GSM8K, where the tax is zero and the recovery is nearly
total. A deployment choosing between KVCOMM and BCR should therefore ask first whether its
entry tokenizations coincide; where they do not, the assembly path is the cheaper thing to fix.

NEGATIVE AND NULL RESULTS
We record the directions the measurements did not support, so that they need not be re-attempted.
The coin’s five seeds.
§4.3 withdraws a null that one coin run supported. The five runs score
223, 231, 236, 246 and 248 of 300 matched GSM8K problems—74.3, 77.0, 78.7, 82.0 and 82.7%,
mean 236.8 or 78.9%, the value Table 5 reports. The draft-scored repair scores 253, above all five;
the withdrawn single run scored 247, above four of them. A policy whose coin advances once per
repaired call can land anywhere in that nine-point band, so one run bounds it from neither side.
Layout of the repair: five variants.
§4.3 attributes the gap between BCR-grow and DENSE on
GSM8K to the unit of repair—BCR-chunk closes it there—which makes a finer or a cleverer unit
the natural next step. We tried both on HumanEval at a fixed row budget (ρ=0.20, so every variant
recomputes the same 140–143 rows per call and only the layout differs), and no variant separates
from a single realisation of the 64-token coin (one run per cell; Table 11 bounds how much such a
cell can show): 16-token chunks by coin, 13/20; the same chunks chosen by draft attention, 16/17;
variable-length spans of 1 to 64 tokens, 25/15. Within the variable-length layout, informed placement
does not beat the same length distribution placed at random (24/16)—an echo of §4.2’s coin result
one level finer. One observation survives as suggestive rather than established: variable-length spans
are nominally above DENSE in all four cells (6/3, 6/3, 8/4, 11/6; none significant, the pooled 31/16
descriptive) and hold that score at ρ=0.10, a third of the rows. A cheaper operating point is reached
through the budget rather than through the layout.
Transplanting CacheBlend.
CacheBlend’s released implementation is a vLLM fork, so repro-
ducing it here meant porting its rule rather than running its server, and the port has a trap worth
recording. Three rules make up the method: rank tokens by the layer-1 value deviation; spend the
budget on the top of that ranking; and recompute, in full and outside the budget, every token of the
request that no cached chunk supplies. The third has no counterpart in this workload. The cache
KVCOMM serves is assembled entirely from stored pieces—a per-agent prefix cache followed by
rotated placeholder and prefix segments—so there is no un-cached tail, and treating the uncovered
positions as one recomputes 74% of the cache: DENSE under CacheBlend’s name. Applying the rule
as written, the budget covers the whole cache.
The distinction matters. With the third rule misapplied, the reproduction scored above DENSE on
HumanEval (7/1 discordant at two agents), which at first read as a property of the workload; with
the rule applied correctly the same cell is within noise of DENSE at either pairing (Table 3). Any
re-use of this comparison should check what the served cache is actually made of before mapping a
chunk-fusion rule onto it.

OPENGATE IMPLEMENTATION
OPENGATE is applied as a runtime patch rather than a fork, so the measured baseline and the
measured method run the same repository code. §4.1 reports it at θ=0.5, the one setting we ran there:
it does not recover what reuse costs, which is the only thing the paper draws from it.
Why generate is the seam.
The patch wraps model.generate. This is the correct seam
because every state mutation in the reuse path—appending the response KV and token ids to shared
memory, updating the anchor bookkeeping—happens after the generate call returns. Raising from
inside generate therefore aborts a reuse attempt without leaving partial state behind, which calling the
public entry point twice would not: a two-token draft issued through the normal interface registers as
a two-token agent response and corrupts every downstream agent’s view of the conversation.
On a call with an assembled cache.
On a call that carries an assembled cache, the wrapper
generates k tokens with output_scores, computes the mean top-1 probability, accepts the draft

when it is at least θ, and either raises (rejection, caught by a wrapper on generate_for_agent
which re-dispatches to the dense path) or continues generation from the draft’s returned cache with
the attention mask extended by k. Calls without a cache—dense prefills and the single-token segment
probes—are passed through untouched.
The θ=0 plumbing control.
The θ=0 control, reported only here, exists to test exactly this
plumbing: with every draft accepted, the draft-then-continue path must be indistinguishable from
single-pass generation under greedy decoding. It agrees with γ=1.0 on all 60 problems individually,
not merely in aggregate accuracy.

LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
Sample size.
The matched-pair protocol of §4 proved valuable in four cases. At the smaller
evaluation size, reuse appeared nearly free, the answer-side gate appeared consistently dominant,
the structural policy appeared beneficial, and a 14.6% latency gain appeared measurable. Each
observation was subsequently tested at n=300 or under a matched control, yielding the more robust
results reported in this paper. Across these cases, the key distinction was the number of discordant
pairs rather than the p-value alone: a clean b=0 among three discordant problems occurs with
probability one in eight under the null. We therefore report discordant counts alongside significance
tests throughout and recommend using roughly twenty or more paired observations when drawing
conclusions from such comparisons.
Latency.
At 4096-symbol segments, KVCOMM reaches the first token 7.6× sooner than DENSE,
while BCR achieves a 2.9× speedup, with the latency premium scaling linearly with ρ and thus
remaining tunable (App. C). On the three benchmarks considered here, neither speedup translates
directly into wall-clock gains: GSM8K’s shared segment averages 59 tokens, so the entire prefill reuse
opportunity accounts for only ≈!100,ms of a ≈!28,s request. This reflects the workload characteristics
rather than a limitation of either method. The two approaches therefore offer complementary accuracy–
latency trade-offs: deployments that can accommodate the accuracy loss in Table 3 can exploit
KVCOMM’s full speedup, while BCR retains 2.9× of the speedup and restores GSM8K accuracy to
the DENSE level.
Where the evidence extends.
Three benchmarks, two backbones, one topology, one chunk size,
and a budget sweep on one cell establish a consistent evaluation foundation; anchor-pool capacity
and the eviction window are held at the repository defaults. These settings define natural axes for
extending the findings, all of which can be explored directly with the existing harness. The n=300
comparisons against DENSE are significant, and the cost effect has the same direction across every
cell and measurement, providing a stable basis for studying its magnitude. The next step is therefore
to characterize how this magnitude changes with the gate, sharpening the cost–accuracy trade-off
rather than establishing its direction. To make these results readily interpretable and reproducible,
reuse rates are consistently reported together with the gate (App. H) and averaging window (§4.1,
Table 6); this reporting convention also provides a clear template for extending the evaluation to the
remaining axes.
Future work.
Three directions follow directly from the results. Significance beyond one run. The
pinned GSM8K run at n=300 provides a direct test, while the reference grid orders the methods
consistently across every column of Table 3. The natural next step is to validate this consistent
ordering cell by cell, using pinned n=300 runs on the remaining benchmarks and backbones; because
the grid cells re-run the same problems, distinct items are needed for an independent test (§4). On
MMLU, the same evaluation can directly test whether a budget recovers the 7.6 points present in
the cache while holding the tokenization term fixed. The pinned run already recovers 3.0 points at
p = 0.16, providing an encouraging signal, while the offline grid does not separate the conditions
sufficiently to resolve the question. A larger pinned evaluation can therefore turn this promising
observation into a definitive measurement.
Generality. BCR assumes only an assembled cache and a seam at which to draft, making its protocol—
matched budget, coin control, and token-matched control—directly applicable to any cross-context
reuse method that concatenates per-segment ids. The same protocol also extends naturally to the

topologies, chunk sizes, and anchor-pool settings held fixed here. Carrying the three quantities
measured in this paper—the cost of reuse, its tokenization share, and the value of the draft—across
these systems provides a direct and systematic test of how broadly the findings transfer. The existing
harness already supports this extension, making broader validation a natural next step.
Latency. The gap between 2.9× and 7.6× is primarily an engineering opportunity rather than a
fundamental limitation: a cheaper recompute path, an adaptive budget that shrinks as the anchor
pool matures, and workloads with long shared segments or many calls per request can increase the
end-to-end benefit when prefill represents a sufficiently large share of the request (App. F). Within the

position-indexed offset does not currently express, and a more informative random control at the
single-row unit, where §4.2 shows that a selector carries roughly five times the value it carries at the
chunk level. Together, these directions provide concrete opportunities to improve both the efficiency
and resolution of the repair mechanism.
