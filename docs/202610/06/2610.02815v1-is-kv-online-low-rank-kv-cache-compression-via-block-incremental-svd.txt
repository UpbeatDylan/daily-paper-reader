ABSTRACT
Long chain-of-thought reasoning substantially increases KV-cache memory dur-
ing autoregressive decoding, as every generated token introduces new key and
value states and causes the cache to grow linearly with decoding length. Exist-
ing KV-cache compression methods typically control this growth through token
eviction, but irreversible deletion can remove historical states that later reason-
ing may need to revisit. SVD-based low-rank compression provides an alterna-
tive by retaining all positions with a more compact representation. However, ex-
tending it from a fixed prompt cache to online decoding is non-trivial. Through
our investigation, we find that if the basis is updated for new tokens while old
tokens keep their coordinates in the old basis, the stored history drifts substan-
tially. Based on this observation, we propose iS-KV, an online low-rank KV-
cache compression method for long-horizon reasoning. iS-KV keeps a recent
window exact while incrementally folding older states into bounded-rank repre-
sentations. As the low-rank basis evolves, it synchronizes historical coordinates
with the updated basis to maintain representation consistency. On DeepSeek-
R1-Distill-Llama-8B, iS-KV achieves 82.6% accuracy at 4.06× persistent-KV
compression, close to the original model’s 83.6%. On Qwen3-8B, it achieves
89.2% accuracy at 5.64× compression. Under matched memory budgets, iS-KV
consistently outperforms token-eviction baselines.

INTRODUCTION
Long chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning increases the memory needed during generation. Each gen-
erated token adds key and value states to the KV cache, so a long reasoning trace can require substan-
tially more KV cache storage than its prompt (Guo et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025). The challenge
is to reduce the KV storage cost during the decoding-phase without losing information that later
reasoning may need.
Two observations motivate our design. First, old tokens can become important again. A token
that receives little attention at the current step may receive strong attention thousands of tokens
later. Once evicted, it cannot be recovered. On Qwen3-8B, for example, R-KV reduces MATH-500
accuracy from 94.4% to 68.8% at about 5.6× compression. Second, KV states have strong low-rank
structure, so old tokens can be compressed instead of deleted. Doing this online requires care: when
the low-rank basis changes as new tokens arrive, the coordinates of previously compressed tokens
must change with it. Otherwise, the stored history no longer represents the same states.
Token-eviction methods reduce cache size by keeping selected positions and removing the rest,
typically using attention scores, recency, or redundancy (Zhang et al., 2023; Xiao et al., 2024; Cai
et al., 2026). These signals describe which tokens appear important under the queries seen so far, but
they cannot predict all future queries. Figure 1(a) illustrates this issue on long reasoning traces from

Low-rank compression offers a different approach: keep every token position, but store each old
state more compactly. Each position is represented by a short coefficient vector over a basis shared
across positions. Figure 1(b) shows that generated KV states are indeed low-rank. Keys have the
fastest singular-value decay before rotary positional embeddings (RoPE), consistent with prior work
(Sun et al., 2025), while values decay more slowly and are harder to compress. The main difficulty
appears during online decoding. As new states arrive, the low-rank basis must adapt. But the
coefficients of old states were computed in the previous basis. If the basis is updated while the old
coefficients remain unchanged, the reconstructed historical states change as well. In other words,
the same coefficients represent different vectors under a new basis. Figure 1(c) measures this drift.
At step 256, updating only the basis gives a drift of 1.55 for keys and 1.40 for values. Updating the
old coefficients together with the basis reduces these values to 0.023 and 0.051. This result shows
that adapting the basis and preserving old states must be handled together.
We therefore propose iS-KV, an online low-rank KV-cache compression method designed for long-
horizon reasoning. iS-KV keeps recent states at their original precision and folds older states into
separate low-rank K and V representations. Using block incremental SVD (Brand, 2006), it updates
both the low-rank basis and the coefficients of all previously compressed positions. Keys are com-
pressed before RoPE, where their low-rank structure is stronger. During attention, the reconstructed
historical states are combined with the recent exact states. Every token position thus remains avail-
able to future queries, although older states are approximate. A fixed rank reduces the storage per
old token, so iS-KV slows cache growth rather than making it constant in size.
On MATH-500, iS-KV achieves 82.6% accuracy at 4.06× persistent-KV compression on DeepSeek-
R1-Distill-Llama-8B, close to the original model’s 83.6%. On Qwen3-8B, it achieves 89.2% at
5.64× compression, compared with 68.8% for matched-memory R-KV. It outperforms both R-KV
and SnapKV at all six matched-memory operating points and remains within 1.0–6.6 accuracy points
of the original model. On AIME 2024 at rank 192, iS-KV on Qwen3-8B matches the original model
with 23 of 30 correct answers, compared with 13 for matched-memory R-KV. Memory matching
counts low-rank factors, exact entries, and R-KV’s staging buffer at the original model’s output
lengths. This avoids counting shorter generations as compression gains. Our contributions are:
2


• Online K/V compression. We develop iS-KV to retain every token position, keeping recent
states exact while storing older states in bounded-rank form.
• Consistent history updates. We show that a basis-only update makes the stored history
drift, and update the basis and old coefficients jointly via block incremental SVD.
• Reasoning accuracy at matched memory. On two 8B models, iS-KV outperforms R-KV
and SnapKV at all six matched MATH-500 operating points. AIME, RULER, ablation, and
latency results further show its strengths and limitations.

RELATED WORK
Token selection and eviction.
Token-selection methods reduce KV cache storage requirement by
keeping a subset of cached positions. H2O retains heavy hitters and recent tokens (Zhang et al.,
2023), while STREAMINGLLM combines attention sinks with a sliding window (Xiao et al., 2024).
FastGen selects head-specific policies through attention profiling (Ge et al., 2024). For long prompts,
SNAPKV selects positions using a trailing observation window (Li et al., 2024), and PyramidKV
allocates different cache budgets across layers (Cai et al., 2024). R-KV targets long reasoning with
redundancy-aware selection (Cai et al., 2026). These methods preserve selected states at the ex-
pense of historical coverage. iS-KV instead keeps every position in exact or low-rank form, trading
representation precision rather than token availability.
Attention efficiency and cache quantization.
Other approaches reduce different parts of the
memory cost. FlashAttention reduces attention IO (Dao et al., 2022); PagedAttention reduces cache
fragmentation and enables sharing (Kwon et al., 2023); GQA reduces the number of KV heads
(Ainslie et al., 2023). Quantization reduces bits per element: KIVI uses per-channel keys and per-
token values (Liu et al., 2023), while KVQuant combines pre-RoPE key quantization with separate
outlier storage (Hooper et al., 2025). Our focus is instead the number of coordinates stored per his-
torical state, without changing the pretrained attention architecture. We do not evaluate additional
low-bit quantization or serving-system integration.
Low-rank KV representations.
Low-rank methods exploit redundancy across features without
discarding token positions. Palu factorizes projection weights and caches low-dimensional inter-
mediate states (Chang et al., 2024); Eigen Attention performs attention in reduced spaces (Saxena
et al., 2024). SHADOWKV combines low-rank pre-RoPE keys, value offloading, and sparse retrieval
(Sun et al., 2025). More recent work learns bases against decoder-layer output error in StiefAtten-
tion (Benfenati et al., 2026), adapts head/block ranks in STAR-KV (Bhatnagar et al., 2026), and
benchmarks data-dependent compressibility in KV-CoRE (Chen et al., 2026). These studies inform
basis construction and rank choice. Our question is how to incorporate newly decoded states while
keeping historical coordinates consistent with an evolving basis.
Online low-rank updates.
For newly generated states, iS-KV maintains a shared historical fac-
torization for each K/V path and updates its basis and old coordinates together. The update uses
Brand’s classical incremental SVD (Brand, 2002; 2006), not a new matrix-decomposition primitive.
Its small core represents the stored history and incoming block before rank truncation (Proposi-
tion 1). Our contribution is this coordinated cache update with exact recent states, evaluated on long
reasoning at matched persistent-memory budgets.

iS-KV compresses the growing KV cache while retaining a representation of every token position.
Prefill initializes exact states and a low-rank history; decoding jointly updates the basis and historical
coefficients as unprotected rows are incorporated in blocks. Figure 2 illustrates the workflow, with
prefill and decoding specified in Algorithms 1 and 2 in Section B.

3.2
BLOCK-INCREMENTAL DECODING
During decoding, new key and value rows remain exact in recent windows of size w. Unprotected
rows leaving these windows remain exact; their positions enter FIFO queues QK for keys and QV
for values. For X ∈{K, V }, QX denotes the corresponding queue. Regular updates consume the
oldest b pending rows once |QX| ≥b, retaining at most rX singular components. Before regular
value updates begin, a one-time refit is performed: once |QV | ≥rV , we append the oldest rV
pending rows to the reconstructed prompt value history and compute a truncated SVD with rank
budget rV . Thus, rV controls both the value rank budget and the initial refit size.

EXPERIMENTS
We evaluate reasoning accuracy, key/value compression and basis adaptation, long-input retrieval,
and generation time and memory.
4.1
LONG-COT REASONING
Models, data, and evaluation.
Our main evaluation uses all 500 MATH-500 questions
(Hendrycks et al., 2021) with DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B (Guo et al., 2025) and Qwen3-8B
(Yang et al., 2025). We report final-answer accuracy from one sampled generation per question,
using temperature 0.6, top-p = 0.95, top-k = 50, and a total prompt-plus-generation limit of 16,384
tokens. Prompts follow each model’s chat template and request a boxed final answer; empty or
non-finite outputs count as incorrect. We compare with the original model and the token-eviction
methods R-KV (Cai et al., 2026) and SnapKV (Li et al., 2024). The original model, R-KV, and iS-
KV use paired questions, prompts, question-specific seeds, and the same answer evaluator. SnapKV
results are retained from the existing baseline summaries; Table 1 distinguishes their inferred correct
counts from directly counted outcomes.
Compression settings and memory matching.
For MATH-500, iS-KV uses ranks r
∈
{64, 96, 128}, a 128-token exact recent window on each path, and 64-row updates. Persistent fac-
tors use FP16, while updates and reconstruction use FP32; further settings are given in Section A.
We match baselines by persistent KV bytes, not by nominal rank or retained-token count. The
accounting includes protected prompt states, exact recent states, low-rank coefficients, singular val-
ues, bases, and baseline staging storage. All footprints are evaluated at the original model’s output
lengths, so different stopping lengths do not change the reference used for matching. These ratios
exclude model weights and temporary reconstruction tensors and therefore do not measure peak
GPU-memory savings.
MATH-500: higher accuracy at matched memory.
Table 1 shows that iS-KV outperforms both
eviction baselines at all six reported model–budget settings. On DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B,
rank 128 reaches 82.6% accuracy at 4.06× compression, compared with 83.6% for the original
model and 78.2% for matched R-KV. At 4.81× and 5.92× compression, the gains over R-KV are 5.4
and 5.6 percentage points, respectively. The advantage also holds on Qwen3-8B: rank 96 achieves
89.2% at 5.64× compression, compared with 68.8% for R-KV and a reported 73.17% for SnapKV.
Even at rank 64, Qwen3-8B retains 87.8% accuracy with 7.12× compression, versus 94.4% for the
original model.
Accuracy degrades gradually as the rank decreases.
Reducing rank from 128 to 64 lowers iS-
KV accuracy by 4.4 percentage points on DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B and 4.2 points on Qwen3-
8B. Over the corresponding matched budgets, R-KV loses 5.6 and 10.6 points, respectively. The
advantage persists across ranks, but the growing gap to the original model shows that retaining
positions alone cannot compensate for lost representation fidelity.

EFFICIENCY
Setup.
We use DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B on an NVIDIA A40 at rank 128 and batch size one.
Both methods receive identical synthetic 8K, 16K, and 32K inputs followed by exactly 1,024 greedy
decode steps. We time prefill plus decoding, excluding model loading, extension compilation, and
cache inspection, and count KV memory after decoding. Each method–length pair has one fresh-
process run, executed serially without inference warmup.
Generation time.
In Figure 4(a), iS-KV takes 47.45, 58.32, and 74.70 s at 8K, 16K, and 32K,
versus 38.83, 49.04, and 68.63 s for the original model. The overhead decreases from 22.2% to
18.9% and 8.8%, including updates and reconstruction. These single-run measurements describe
this workload, not a statistically stable latency difference.
Memory.
In Figure 4(b), the original model requires 1.125, 2.125, and 4.125 GiB at 8K, 16K, and
32K; iS-KV uses 0.746, 1.185, and 1.808 GiB. Savings increase from 33.7% to 44.2% and 56.2% as
more history enters the low-rank representation. This includes factors and exact protected, recent,
and pending rows, preserving all positions without making memory constant in length. It excludes
indices, unused capacity, reconstructed caches, and workspace.

5
CONCLUSION
Long chain-of-thought reasoning grows the KV cache with every generated token. Eviction methods
shrink it by deleting tokens, but a token that looks unimportant now may matter later, and deletion
cannot be undone. iS-KV instead keeps every token position and stores old states compactly, while
recent states stay exact. This lowers the cost of each stored token, though memory still grows with
the length of the trace. The difficulty is doing this online: the compact representation adapts as new
tokens arrive, and the old states are stored relative to it. If it changes while they stay fixed, those
states may not be reconstructed well. iS-KV updates both together with block-incremental SVD, so
every update preserves the stored history. Our takeaway is that compressing a growing cache is a
problem of representing every token faithfully beyond just choosing which tokens to keep.

A.1
METHOD SETTINGS
Main configuration.
The key and value caches use exact recent windows of 128 rows, block size
64, and residual tolerance τ = 10−6. Each update keeps at most the given rank budget. MATH-500
uses ranks 64, 96, and 128 on both models, with all other settings unchanged. Reorthogonalization
runs after every 256 rows processed by regular block updates; these rows include initialization from
an empty history but not the first value refit. The persistent factors UX, BX and the diagonal entries
of ΣX are stored in FP16; projection, residual QR, core SVD, reorthogonalization, and reconstruc-
tion use FP32. Factors are cast back to the storage precision after each update. Each right basis is
stored explicitly, protected prompt keys are stored once, and no additional ΣXB⊤
X product is cached.
Optional features of our code that this paper does not use are disabled: prompt sketches, persistent
residual corrections, dynamic historical-token scoring, and integer quantization.
Prompt protection.
During prefill, we partition post-RoPE keys into eight-token chunks, exclud-
ing the last four chunks and any final incomplete chunk as a local suffix. For each KV head, we score
a chunk by the minimum cosine similarity between its token keys and their chunk mean, then select
the 128 lowest-scoring chunks (or all available chunks for a shorter prompt). We take the union
of their token positions across heads and the local suffix. These positions retain exact pre-RoPE
keys and are excluded from key factorization; all other prompt keys are factorized. Values within 32
tokens of the protected key positions remain protected throughout decoding. The latest 128 prompt
values initialize the exact recent window; values outside both sets are factorized. The 64K RULER
evaluation uses this same rule, so its prompt is only partly represented by low-rank factors.
Cache layout.
The key cache stores pre-RoPE representations. Protected prompt rows remain
exact and are excluded from the compact low-rank coefficient matrix. In the MATH protocol, the
chunk-selection rule normally covers the entire short prompt, which therefore remains exact. De-
coded keys enter an exact FIFO buffer; once it contains 128+64 rows, the oldest 64 are incorporated
into the low-rank history. The value cache retains protected prompt rows and a recent window of
128 rows exactly. The value rank budget rV also determines the number of rows used in the initial
value refit. Once rV unprotected value rows have left the recent window, we append them to the
reconstructed prompt value history and recompute a truncated SVD with rank budget rV . Later
value updates use 64-row blocks. Incomplete blocks remain uncompressed. Recent and eligible
unprotected rows share one exact buffer, with eligibility determined by position. The separate WX
and QX in Algorithms 1 and 2 are logical index lists for these two subsets.
Residual basis construction.
The update schedule is determined by the number of eligible rows,
independently of the residual threshold. For a block F, we first compute E = F −(FB)B⊤and
then replace it by E −(EB)B⊤to reduce residual components along the current basis caused by
finite precision. If ∥E∥F ≤τ or k = d, the residual basis is empty. Otherwise, we compute a
reduced QR factorization E⊤= QERE and an SVD RE = LEDEH⊤
E . Let δ1 ≥δ2 ≥· · · denote
the singular values on the diagonal of DE. The retained residual basis is defined by
τeff = max(τ, ϵ max(b, d)∥F∥F ) ,
p = min(d −k, #{i : δi > τeff}) ,
P = QELE[:, 1 : p],
where ϵ is machine epsilon at the computation precision and τ = 10−6 by default. The lower-right
block of the core matrix is FP, which equals EP when the residual is exact and B⊤P = 0.

Factor precision at a 32K budget.
The persistent factors are FP16 in the MATH-500 runs. At a
32,768-token generation budget on Qwen3-8B they overflow: entries exceed the FP16 maximum of
65,504, become ±∞, and the logits turn into NaN. On the same question, we observed this with three
versions of our code: the version used for the main table, the latest version, and the latest version
with all speed optimizations turned off, at 13K, 19K, and 55K decoded tokens, respectively. FP32
and BF16 factors both completed the same generation, so the failure comes from the representation
rather than from one implementation. The original model and R-KV are unaffected because they
store raw keys and values in BF16, whose largest finite value is about 3.4 × 1038, whereas iS-KV
stores derived factors that are updated incrementally and grow with sequence length.
We therefore store factors in BF16 for all 32K experiments. BF16 occupies the same two bytes as
FP16, so the compression ratios are unchanged; FP32 would cut Qwen3-8B rank-128 compression
from 6.47× to about 3.8×. BF16 gives up three mantissa bits for a much larger range. Because
BF16 and FP16 factors do not give bitwise identical results, we report the 32K results as a sep-
arate configuration and do not rerun MATH-500 in BF16. In the one setting run in both formats,
DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B at rank 128 on AIME 2024, accuracy is the same (56.7%), but BF16
lengthens mean generations from 15,541 to 18,126 tokens and doubles the share of generations that
reach the length limit from 13% to 27%. The lower precision therefore still changes when generation
stops.

Proposition 4 (Block-update cost). For block size b, width d, rank budget r, and n factorized rows,
projection and residual computation require O(bdr) work. Residual basis construction factorizes a
d × b matrix, and the core matrix has at most r + b rows and columns. Updating the historical coef-
ficients costs O(nr(r + b)). Persistent factor storage is O((n + d)r). Periodic reorthogonalization
adds work that depends on the number n of historical rows.
A.5
NUMERICAL DIAGNOSTICS
Residual threshold.
These probes test the numerical fidelity of the update, not the gap to the
original KV cache or generation quality. With an invalid, oversized residual threshold, value drift
reaches 0.961 (step 1,024, layer 0) and 0.906 (step 2,048, layer 15); the stable update gives 0.0001
and 0.0109 at the same points.

The prefill initialization and per-step decoding procedures summarized in Section 3 are specified

Prefill initialization (Algorithm 1).
Algorithm 1 realizes the construction of Section 3.1. It first
evaluates exact causal attention over the prompt, so the prefill outputs never depend on the com-
pressed representation. It then selects the protected key positions PK from the chunk outlier scores
sc,h of Section 3, taking the union of the selected chunks with the local suffix and dilating them by
ρ positions to form the protected value positions PV ; the last min(w, L) prompt positions initialize
the exact recent windows WK and WV . For each of K and V , the exact set EX = PX ∪WX is
stored verbatim and the remaining positions CX = I \ EX are factorized by the truncated SVD
SVDrX of Section 3, retaining kX = min(rX, |CX|, d) components. The pending queues QX and
update counters cX start empty, and the flag fV records that the one-time value refit of Section 3.2
is still pending.

Decoding step (Algorithm 2).
Algorithm 2 maintains the cache of Section 3.2 during generation.
For each of the key and value streams it stores the arriving row exactly, advances the recent window,
and enqueues the unprotected positions that leave it. A fold is triggered once the queue holds a
